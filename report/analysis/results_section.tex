\subsection{Experimental Setup and Scoring}

The tournament contained 81,216 simulations: 27,072 configurations, each repeated with three seeds. It included nine groups, households of 1, 2, 5, 9, 18, and 36 roommates, both \(u=4\) and \(u=5\), four drawer multipliers, and twelve budget and duration settings. The settings ranged from no replacement money to an unlimited budget, with durations from one to ten years. The raw file contains 578,016 roommate records and no recorded player faults. \oldtext{These results evaluate the tournament submission; they do not separately measure the contributions of our earlier strategies or individual components of the final strategy.} \newtext{Section~\ref{sec:ablation} adds one controlled experiment: we replay the tournament households in which Group 4 held most of the seats with our voluntary discards switched off.}

For each group present in a simulation, we first average its roommates' outcomes. Unless stated otherwise, we then give each simulation equal weight. Thus, eight copies of a group in one household do not count as eight independent experiments. Each group appears in 29,376 simulations. The supplied standings instead average individual roommate rows, giving more weight to runs with more copies of a group. Appendix~\ref{app:weighting} reports both conventions. All plots are descriptive averages over the tested settings. With only three repetitions per configuration, we do not interpret small differences as precise rankings of strategy quality.

To separate matching quality from shortages, let \(E\) be a roommate's total embarrassment and \(N_0\) its number of sockless days. We calculate
\[
E_{\mathrm{match}}=E-256^2N_0.
\]
The daily score is \(E/T\), and the mismatch component is \(E_{\mathrm{match}}/T\). We also report mismatch embarrassment per dressed day by dividing the sum of mismatch embarrassment by the sum of \(T-N_0\) across observations. This avoids treating a day without socks as successful matching. Household spending and the first day on which a replacement purchase failed are counted once per simulation. A failed purchase does not necessarily imply a subsequent sockless turn: enough socks may remain to finish the run.

\newtext{\subsection{What We Tested Before the Tournament}}

\newtext{Our development tests covered a narrower range of households than the tournament. The future-hand discard rule (Section~\ref{sec:small-budget}) was accepted on four roommates sharing \(C=40\) socks for 1{,}080 days with \(B=\$800\) (\(\rho=18\)); over 20 seeds it lowered mean embarrassment from 6.96 to 0.18 per roommate-day. The final changes to the budget controller were tested in every two- and four-player lineup of the nine groups with \(C=40\), \(u=4\), 1{,}080 days and \(B=\$7{,}490\) (\(\rho=1.9\), a large budget). Over 8{,}568 simulations they lowered Group 4's mean embarrassment by 20.3\% (95\% interval 6.4--32.9\%), leaving Group 4 with the lowest field mean, statistically tied with Group 8. No test had more than nine roommates in a household.}

\newtext{Every finite budget in the tournament had \(\rho\ge 3.6\), so the large-budget rule (Section~\ref{sec:large-budget}) ran only in the unlimited-budget games, and the small-budget rule decided every other game, including households of 18 and 36 roommates.}

\subsection{Overall Performance: Shortages Dominate the Score}

Figure~\ref{fig:overall} and Table~\ref{tab:overall} show that Group 4 was near the bottom under equal simulation weighting, with mean embarrassment of 923.63 per roommate-day. Its median was only 0.035, illustrating that most runs had very small scores while a minority incurred severe penalties. Group 4 experienced at least one sockless turn in 5.33\% of its simulations, and sockless days accounted for 99.95\% of its mean score. Preventing relatively rare availability failures would therefore have a much larger effect on this aggregate score than a comparable improvement in ordinary matching. \newtext{Ranked within each of the 1{,}152 configurations, Group 4 placed first in 325 and last in 249.}

This does not mean our matching policy was best. Its mismatch embarrassment per dressed day was 0.689, the highest among the nine groups, compared with 0.471 for Group 8. However, the scale of these differences is small compared with the shortage penalty. \oldtext{Both components matter: a policy can match well while socks are available and still perform poorly over the full horizon.}

\subsection{Budget, Duration, and Household Size}

Figure~\ref{fig:budget} restricts attention to households containing only Group 4 players. Each cell averages both draw sizes, all four drawer multipliers, and the three repetitions. Group 4 had no sockless days in any single-roommate run and averaged only 0.010 embarrassment per day in that setting. Larger households were much more vulnerable, but the household budget \(B\) was not increased with \(n\). Increasing \(n\) consequently reduced both budget per roommate-year and, at a fixed drawer multiplier and draw size, initial socks per roommate. The comparison does not isolate a cost of coordination.

Duration also matters. With nine Group 4 roommates and \(B=150\), one-year simulations averaged 0.496 embarrassment per day and had no sockless runs. Extending the same budget to two years increased the mean to 4,806.05, with sockless days in 41.7\% of runs. Even the largest finite budget, \(B=1500\), did not guarantee survival: over ten years, all 36-roommate homogeneous Group 4 runs had sockless days. Conversely, all homogeneous Group 4 runs with unlimited replacement money had zero embarrassment. \oldtext{The main failures therefore occurred under resource limits, although low mismatch scores under unlimited replacement do not establish that the same replacement behavior is economical.} \newtext{Across all households with unlimited money, where the large-budget rule ran, Group 4 placed first in 82 of 96 configurations.}

\subsection{Replacement Pressure and Budget Exhaustion}

Figure~\ref{fig:exhaustion} compares homogeneous households using \(\rho=10nT/(3B)\), the replacement pressure defined in Section~\ref{sec:notation}. Higher \(\rho\) means each purchased sock must support more wears. The figure includes only positive finite budgets. Outcomes at each pressure value are averaged over the compatible tested settings, so duration and initial stock still matter within this comparison.

Across all homogeneous Group 4 runs, a replacement purchase failed in 30.9\%, whereas sockless days occurred in 22.8\%. In 8.1\% of runs, purchases failed but the remaining drawer lasted through the horizon. \oldtext{Exhaustion is therefore a useful warning rather than an interchangeable measure of failure.} Its timing matters as well: an early failed purchase leaves more time for holes and discards to deplete the remaining stock. \oldtext{The high-pressure portion of Figure~\ref{fig:exhaustion} shows earlier purchase failures and a larger fraction of sockless days. Other groups also struggled in this region, indicating that some tested budgets were broadly difficult, while Group 4's additional losses show that its safeguards did not fully control this risk.} \newtext{Group 4 households were the first to run out of money as pressure rose. At \(\rho=72\) and \(\rho=86\), their budgets ran out in 53\% and 75\% of runs, against at most 28\% and 38\% for any other group. Above \(\rho\approx100\), every group ran out of money in most runs, and Group 4 spent the most roommate-days without socks at every pressure level but one.}

\newtext{\subsection{Voluntary Discards Caused the Excess Shortages}\label{sec:ablation}}

\newtext{To test whether our discards caused these failures, we replayed every finite-budget tournament simulation in which Group 4 held most of the seats, and the nine-person households with a single Group 4 player: 5{,}280 simulations in total. Each replay kept the roster, seat order, drawer, budget, duration and seed. The only change was that Group 4's discard allowance (Section~\ref{sec:discard-number}) was always zero; worn-out socks with holes were still replaced. Forty randomly chosen simulations replayed with the unmodified player reproduced every roommate's tournament score exactly.}

\newtext{Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} show the effect. In the 18-roommate all-Group-4 households, mean embarrassment fell from 7{,}221 to 5{,}923 per roommate-day and the budget ran out in 45\% of runs instead of 69\%. Among the nine groups' means in those households, Group 4 moved from last to third. With nine copies, or eight Group 4 players and one other group, Group 4 moved from eighth to sixth and from eighth to second. In the two-person households the budget covered both holes and discards, and there the discards helped: switching them off raised mean embarrassment from 49.7 to 50.6.}

\newtext{Replacing the replayed simulations in the standings, with every roommate's score taken from the replay, lowers Group 4's mean from 923.63 to 887.92. That would rank second, behind Group 1 at 886.58; the other groups' means also fall slightly because they share some of the replayed households. Almost all of the gap in Figure~\ref{fig:households} therefore comes from voluntary discards in households whose budget could not pay for them.}

\subsection{The Influence of Other Roommates}

Figure~\ref{fig:neighbors} compares Group 4 with each other group under the same budget, duration, draw-size, and drawer-multiplier grid. In two-person households, its mean score ranged from 30.40 with Group 2 to 63.91 with Group 10. Yet every partner produced the same 1.04\% frequency of runs with Group 4 sockless days. Differences in total score can therefore reflect the severity and duration of shortages, even when their frequency is unchanged.

In nine-person households, a single Group 4 player among eight Group 9 players averaged 1,223.54 embarrassment per day, compared with 991.84 among eight Group 3 players. When eight players used Group 4 and only one used another policy, Group 4 averaged 1,353.56 with a Group 9 neighbor. These results show sensitivity to household composition, but the best neighbor depends on the metric: among eight Group 7 players, Group 4's mismatch component was only 0.418 per day, compared with 1.215 among eight Group 1 players. \oldtext{A neighbor that supports close matching need not minimize shortage risk.}

The raw file records household spending, not individual discard decisions or individual expenditure. We therefore cannot identify which roommate caused a particular purchase failure\newtext{\ from the tournament records alone; the replay in Section~\ref{sec:ablation} addresses this for households dominated by Group 4}. Appendix~\ref{app:externalities} extends this comparison to changes in the outcomes of other groups when Group 4 enters a five-person household.

\subsection{Initial Drawer Capacity Can Delay or Prevent Shortages}

Figure~\ref{fig:capacity} varies drawer capacity within fixed household types and budget-duration settings. For 36 Group 4 roommates with \(B=300\) over one year, doubling the base drawer reduced mean embarrassment from 12,898.96 to 1.85 and eliminated sockless days in the tested runs. Initial capacity can consequently determine whether a household survives a short horizon even when replacement money per roommate is small.

The protection was incomplete over longer horizons. With 36 Group 4 roommates and \(B=1500\) over ten years, increasing the drawer multiplier from 1 to 10 reduced sockless roommate-days from 73.0\% to 36.5\%, but did not remove shortages. A large initial drawer is a finite buffer, so its contribution becomes smaller relative to total wear as \(T\) grows. The mixed 36-person households showed a similar pattern.

More capacity also did not improve ordinary matching monotonically. In nine-person homogeneous households with \(B=300\) over one year, there were no sockless days at any drawer multiplier, but daily embarrassment increased from 0.139 at multiplier 1 to 0.373 at multiplier 2 before falling to 0.041 at multiplier 10. Capacity changes the evolving shade distribution as well as the available stock; the aggregate records cannot determine the exact mechanism behind this pattern.

\subsection{Drawing Four Versus Five Socks}

We matched the \(u=4\) and \(u=5\) runs by roster, budget, duration, drawer multiplier, and seed, giving 14,688 paired comparisons for Group 4. Figure~\ref{fig:unit} shows an improvement at every household size. Across these pairs, mean daily embarrassment fell from 1,071.10 to 776.16, a reduction of 27.5\%. The mismatch component fell from 0.751 to 0.084 per roommate-day, and sockless roommate-days fell from 1.63\% to 1.18\%. Thus, the improvement included both better matching and greater availability. \newtext{Relative to the field, Group 4 also did better with five socks: its mean rank within configurations was 4.44 with \(u=5\), against 5.56 with \(u=4\).}

These comparisons evaluate the tested five-sock setup as a whole. Its base drawer was also larger: for example, with nine roommates it contained 60 socks instead of 48, and with 36 roommates it contained 192 instead of 156. There were no paired runs holding the actual drawer capacity \(C\) constant while changing \(u\). The extra candidate sock increases the number of possible pairs from six to ten, but these data cannot separate that advantage from the benefit of the larger drawer.
